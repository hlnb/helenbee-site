July does not arrive with the clean confidence of January.

January has a shine to it. New pages. Fresh intentions. That slightly dangerous belief that if I just choose the right system, the right word, the right set of goals, the year will behave itself.

By July, the year has usually stopped pretending.

Some things have moved. Some things have stalled. Some things have quietly fallen off the edge of the table. The appointments have happened. The energy has fluctuated. The routines have held in some places and frayed in others. Life has interrupted the plan, as life tends to do, with very little respect for colour-coding.

This year, I did not want my mid-year reset to become a quarterly business review in a cardigan.

I did not want to sit down with my planners and journals and ask, "Where have I failed?"

I wanted to ask a better question.

What is still helping me live steadily? And what has become another thing asking to be managed?

There is a difference.

A reset can sound gentle while still behaving like self-criticism. It can become another way of measuring ourselves against an imagined version of the year. The one who had perfect energy, perfect discipline, no medical appointments, no emotional weather, no community obligations, no fatigue, and no need to sit quietly for a while before continuing.

January often makes plans with a version of us who has not yet lived through February, March, April, May, or June.

July knows more.

So I sat down with the actual evidence of my days: my main planner, my journal, my working planner in its rings binder, my digital systems, my routines, the trackers I had kept, and the ones I had quietly abandoned.

I was not looking for a verdict. I was looking for information.

In my main planner, I have a quarterly overview section. It holds my quarterly priorities and the goals connected to the main pillars of my life: health and wellbeing, professional, personal, and community. That part still works. It gives me direction without asking for too much detail.

It reminds me what season I am in.

The monthly overview has a different job. That is where I place all my appointments for the month, along with the scheduled things that shape my rhythm: gym, Pilates, RA Social, medical appointments, and the ordinary life things that need to be seen before I make promises to myself or anyone else.

That matters because an appointment-heavy month is not an empty month.

It may not produce the same kind of visible output as a writing month or a tutorial-building month, yet it still takes energy. It takes preparation, travel, transition, recovery, decision-making, and sometimes the emotional load of being a body in a medical system.

Seeing those commitments laid out across the month helps me notice the rhythm I am actually living inside.

The weekly layout then gives me the closer view. I mark scheduled things with placeholders, not because I need to control every hour, but because I need to see what is already spoken for. I have a habits section for medication, journaling, meditation, and facial care. I record my sleep score, although I have decided to simplify that too. Instead of recording both Withings and Garmin, I am only using Withings going forward.

That small decision says something larger.

I do not need maximum information. I need useful information.

Wearables and trackers can be helpful. They can show patterns we might otherwise miss. They can also become another quiet authority figure, especially when every score starts to feel like a judgement. Research and clinical commentary on over-monitoring and "over-optimisation" point to this tension: health data can support awareness, but constant tracking can also create stress, mental fatigue, and a disconnection from our own internal cues. ([Real Simple](https://www.realsimple.com/over-optimization-backlash-12009274))

I know that feeling. There is a particular absurdity in waking up tired and then checking a device to see whether I am allowed to feel tired.

So for July, I changed the question. Instead of chasing a perfect sleep score, I am more interested in whether I gave myself the opportunity for seven to eight hours sleep. That is a gentler measure. It does not pretend I can control every stage of sleep. It simply asks whether I protected the conditions for rest.

That is the kind of reset I am interested in now.

Less judgement. More relationship.

I also reviewed what I had been tracking as hobbies: reading, knitting, TV watching, YouTube, learning, and drinking water. I noticed that water probably belongs more with habits than hobbies. The others are less about productivity and more about nourishment, regulation, input, and enjoyment.

Reading and knitting are not just pleasant extras. They help me return to myself. Learning keeps my mind engaged. Even TV and YouTube can tell me something about the kind of rest I am reaching for, whether it is genuine decompression, background noise, curiosity, or avoidance.

The point is not to moralise every activity. The point is to notice.

Some tracking helps me see patterns. Some tracking becomes overhead. When a system costs more than it returns, that is not failure. That is data.

That was one of the clearest lessons of this review. Some things were being recorded in more than one place. Exercise appeared here and there. Reading, routines, meditation, appointments, and learning were all useful things, but not all of them needed to be reported to multiple committees.

A planner can become a tiny bureaucracy if I am not careful.

Very neat. Very earnest. Deeply annoying.

The biggest change has been in my working planner and journal, which is a rings binder. I have tried several layouts in it over the first half of the year, looking for something that gave me enough structure without turning every day into a command centre.

For July, I am trialling a layout similar in spirit to Take A Note planners. On one side, I list the non-negotiables for Monday through Sunday: appointments, scheduled items, and fixed commitments. On the other side, I record the tasks I actually complete during studio time in a weekly-style setup.

I am deliberately not using it for strict time-blocking.

That matters.

Time-blocking can be useful, but for me it can also become brittle. It assumes the day will behave. It assumes energy will arrive at the appointed hour, that appointments will not take more out of me than expected, that creative work will fit neatly into boxes, and that transition time is somehow not real time.

My life does not work like that.

So the new layout is less about controlling the week and more about staying in relationship with it.

The fixed points are visible. The studio time is visible. The tasks completed are visible. Instead of asking, "Did I follow the plan perfectly?" the page asks, "What was possible, and what moved forward?"

That feels kinder. It also feels more honest.

One of the most important changes has been renaming my HelenBurgess and GraphiteEdge work as "studio time."

This might seem small from the outside. It has not felt small from the inside.

The word "work" had started to feel heavy, especially around social media creation and scheduling. It made writing, teaching, content planning, tutorial development, website care, and creative thinking feel like a set of obligations waiting to be processed.

"Studio time" changes the emotional texture. It gives the work a room. It reminds me that this is not only output. It is practice. It is making. It is thinking. It is tending. It is the place where my writing, teaching, technical skills, and reflective life meet.

I am not reporting for duty. I am entering the studio.

That distinction matters more than I expected.

My daily journal has also become clearer. On one side of the page, I list the routine things: medications, meditation, Claude CoWork, Notion, email, and the book or books I am reading. These are the small hinges that help the day open properly.

On the other side, I list appointments, scheduled events I need to be aware of, and any tasks I need to do during studio time.

This split helps me separate maintenance from movement.

Medication and meditation are not the same kind of task as writing an article or creating a tutorial. Checking email is not the same as building a social media campaign. Reading is not just a hobby floating around the edges; it is part of how I orient myself.

Some things keep the human online. Some things move the work forward. Both matter, but they ask different things of me.

The digital side of the system has changed too. I use Claude CoWork to review my calendar, email, and Notion, then help identify the tasks that belong to each themed day. It acts as a kind of external scaffold. It reduces the need to hold everything in my head before I have even begun.

This is not about outsourcing my life to technology. It is about reducing cognitive load so I can use my actual capacity for the things that matter.

That distinction is important. Tools should support attention, not scatter it. They should help me begin the day with reality in view, not with the dangerous little fiction that "I'll fit it in somewhere."

One of the biggest lessons from the first half of the year came from experimenting with a four-hour workday.

On paper, I had a four-hour workday. In practice, I often did not have four hours of focused work.

Appointments, scheduled exercise, medical commitments, RA Social, transition time, and the ordinary admin of having a body and a life often truncated the available focus time. The day might have contained four hours labelled as work, but that did not mean it contained four hours of actual focused studio time.

That was not a discipline problem. It was a design problem.

So I have adjusted the scheduled task and artifact in Claude CoWork to make the aim clearer: each day needs to protect four hours of focused studio time. Not four hours minus appointments. Not four hours in theory. Not four hours that disappears before I reach the desk.

Four hours of actual focus.

That is the time I need for social media creation, writing, tutorial development, and the deeper work that does not happen well in scraps.

Focus time does not appear because I value it. It appears because I protect it.

This is where the mental health part of the reset becomes very real. A system that continually asks me to create from leftover time will eventually make the creative work feel like pressure. It turns meaningful work into something I am always trying to squeeze in around the edges.

That is not sustainable.

Research on psychological detachment from work suggests that the ability to mentally switch off during non-work time is an important part of recovery, and is linked with wellbeing and lower emotional exhaustion. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11731735/)) Rest is not an indulgence sitting outside the system. It is part of what makes the system possible.

The same is true of compassionate adjustment. Research on self-compassion and perfectionism suggests that self-compassion can help buffer the link between perfectionism and psychological distress, including anxiety, insomnia, social dysfunction, and depressive symptoms. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10669294/))

That makes sense to me. Harshness has never made me more whole. It may produce short bursts of compliance, but it does not create steadiness. It does not help me build a life I can keep living inside.

There is a kind of honesty that does not bruise. That is what I want this reset to practise.

Not the honesty of "you should have done more."

The honesty of "this is what your life is actually asking of you."

A mid-year reset can easily become another form of productivity guilt. Another opportunity to measure the gap between the imagined self and the actual one. Another way to ask whether rest has been earned. Expert commentary on productivity guilt makes the same point in plain terms: tying worth to constant output can make rest feel like failure, even though rest is essential for emotional wellbeing and recovery. ([Verywell Mind](https://www.verywellmind.com/how-to-overcome-productivity-guilt-and-embrace-rest-12010180))

I am trying to unlearn that.

Not perfectly. Not in a single dramatic July gesture.

Slowly. Practically. With planners and journals and renamed sections and fewer duplicate trackers and a better definition of enough.

The Stoics would probably not be surprised by any of this. At the heart of Stoic practice is the distinction between what is within our control and what is not. Not as a slogan. As a discipline.

I do not control every health fluctuation. I do not control every appointment date. I do not control other people's expectations, administrative delays, energy levels, or whether a week unfolds neatly.

I do have some say over boundaries. I have some say over pace. I have some say over what I call things, where I place things, how I define enough, and whether I keep building my days around imaginary capacity.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy uses a related idea through the language of psychological flexibility: the ability to stay present with reality and act in ways that are consistent with personal values, rather than being ruled by rigid patterns or avoidance. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11837766/))

That is what this reset feels like to me.

Not starting again from nothing. Not becoming a new person in July. Listening honestly to the person I have been becoming.

The first half of the year has shown me that I do need structure. I like structure. Structure helps me write, teach, recover, manage websites, support community work, and return to the things that matter.

But I need humane structure.

Structure that can see the appointments. Structure that knows exercise counts. Structure that does not treat health care as an interruption to life, but as part of life. Structure that understands social media creation is still creative labour, even when it arrives wearing the unflattering hat of admin. Structure that lets studio time feel like a place of practice, not a punishment.

I want the second half of the year to feel steady, hopeful, rested, and capable.

Steady, not rushed.

Hopeful, not falsely cheerful.

Rested, not because everything is finished, but because rest has been built into the way things continue.

Capable, not because I am proving myself to everyone, but because I am trusting the skills, lived experience, recovery work, and quiet persistence that are already here.

I do not want the second half of the year to look impressive from the outside and feel punishing from the inside.

That is the line I keep coming back to.

It is tempting to measure a year by what can be shown: articles published, tutorials created, websites maintained, posts scheduled, tasks completed, appointments attended, boxes ticked.

Some of that matters. But the inner measure matters too.

Did the system help me stay well enough to keep going? Did it make room for the body I actually have? Did it support the mind I am actually living with? Did it help me create without turning every act of creation into another test? Did it leave space for reading, knitting, learning, rest, and ordinary human maintenance? Did it help me return to myself?

My reset is not a dramatic reinvention.

It is a quiet decision to stop asking my systems to supervise me and start asking them to support me.

July does not ask me to start again from nothing. It asks me to begin again from here.

Older than January. Wiser than January. A little more tired, perhaps. Also more honest.
